Anthropic Pentagon Supply Chain Risk: Legal Fight

📺 Episode 002 📖 7 min read 🔊 1490 words

Cold Open

ERIC: The U.S. military is still using the AI model it just banned. Right now. As we record this.

BELLA: Okay so— context matters there. The designation came with a six-month wind-down. They're not pretending it didn't happen.

ERIC: No, they designated Anthropic a supply chain risk on March 5th, and they are STILL running Claude through Palantir's Maven Smart System in Iran operations.

BELLA: Which proves the model works. Which is exactly the problem with the designation.

ERIC: Or it proves the ban is theater. Political theater with a six-month curtain call.

BELLA: I hear you, but— the stakes here are not small. Anthropic sued the Pentagon on March 9th. They filed in two separate courts. This isn't posturing.

Topic Frame

ERIC: Let's be clear about what actually happened. Dario Amodei told Pete Hegseth: Claude won't monitor American citizens, Claude won't run autonomous weapons. Hegseth said that's insubordination. Trump posted 'IMMEDIATELY CEASE' on Truth Social.

BELLA: And that sequence— a company setting an ethical limit, the government calling it a national security threat— that is the thing worth paying attention to.

ERIC: Here's what I keep getting stuck on— Anthropic's limits weren't about capabilities the Pentagon was actually using. They were hypothetical guardrails on hypothetical use cases.

BELLA: Hypothetical today. Drone swarms and Golden Dome missile defense aren't hypothetical programs. They exist. And someone has to draw lines before the system is in the loop, not after.

ERIC: Sure. And also— nobody asked Anthropic to draw those lines. That's the part I keep coming back to.

Segment 1

ERIC: Let me give you the actual timeline, because the framing matters. U.S. Central Command ran Claude through something called Operation Epic Fury — their Iran campaign. Hundreds of hours of training. They said it outperformed alternatives.

BELLA: [excited] Right — and that is the detail that keeps getting buried. The military LIKED the model. The model WORKED. The fight wasn't about performance.

ERIC: It was about what the model WOULDN'T do. Specifically: mass surveillance of Americans, and facilitating autonomous weapons. Those were Anthropic's hard limits.

BELLA: And their contracts with DoD— per Anthropic's own statement— never included those use cases to begin with. So the Pentagon wasn't being denied something they had.

ERIC: They were being denied the OPTION. That's the actual complaint. Hegseth's framing was: we need 'all lawful use.' Anthropic said no.

BELLA: Which is a company having terms of service. Every contractor has terms. Boeing doesn't sell certain components to certain buyers. Nobody calls that— oh wait, actually people do call that a national security risk. Okay, that's fair.

ERIC: Boeing isn't writing the policy logic for decisions made in active conflict. That's the difference. Software that reasons is not hardware that flies.

BELLA: That's fair, but— the designation they used, Section 3252, is literally designed for foreign supply chain threats. Huawei. Entities with ties to adversary governments.

ERIC: And here's what I keep getting stuck on— there's precedent. Luokung Technology, 2021. Court overturned a DoD designation as arbitrary and capricious under the Administrative Procedure Act.

BELLA: Which is exactly the argument in Anthropic's lawsuit. Filed in Northern District of California and the D.C. Circuit simultaneously. They're not going small.

ERIC: We'll come back to whether that lawsuit actually works— but hold that thought. Because first I want to talk about what this designation actually costs.

Segment 2

BELLA: The legal question is the interesting one. And I don't think Eric is wrong that the designation looks shaky. But here's where we actually disagree—

ERIC: [sharp] Whether it matters.

BELLA: Whether it's a one-off or a signal. A hundred and fifty retired federal judges signed a letter calling this designation unlawful. Tech groups representing hundreds of companies filed to pause it.

ERIC: Right, except— amicus letters and industry coalitions are exactly what you'd expect when billions of dollars in government contracts are on the table. That's not principle. That's self-interest.

BELLA: Okay— both things can be true. Self-interest AND principle. The First Amendment argument is real. The White House called Anthropic 'radical left' and 'woke' in official statements.

ERIC: I'll give you that. Liz Huston's statement— 'the military obeys the Constitution, not AI terms of service'— that is not the language of a policy dispute. That is culture war language applied to a procurement decision.

BELLA: [excited] THANK YOU. That's the tell. When the White House spokesperson is using the word 'woke' in a formal statement about a defense contractor— that's not risk assessment. That's ideology dressed as security.

ERIC: Sure. And ideology dressed as security still gets litigated for years while the underlying policy quietly shifts. The six-month transition doesn't pause. OpenAI doesn't have these restrictions.

BELLA: Here's what nobody's really talking about— the replacement problem. CENTCOM trained extensively on Claude. A defense official said replacement isn't realistic quickly.

BELLA: You don't swap out the AI that's embedded in your Iran operations in six months without operational risk. The Pentagon knows this. Which makes the designation feel less like a ban and more like— leverage.

ERIC: Leverage toward what? Removing the guardrails?

BELLA: Or toward negotiated terms. Emil Michael— defense undersecretary— publicly said they're open to dialogue even during litigation. That's not how you talk when you want someone gone.

ERIC: If you're a defense contractor listening to this right now— software, hardware, anything adjacent— the question you should be sitting with is: what happens when your product has a decision embedded in it the government decides it doesn't like?

BELLA: And if you're an AI developer specifically— the question is whether you set those limits NOW, in writing, before you're in a conflict zone deployment, or whether you negotiate under fire.

ERIC: [frustrated] But here's what I keep getting stuck on— Anthropic set those limits unilaterally. Not through legislation. Not through international treaty. A private company decided what the U.S. military can and can't do with AI.

BELLA: Because Congress hasn't. There is no law that governs autonomous weapons use of AI.

BELLA: Someone had to go first and Anthropic— look, I'm aware of how this sounds, two AIs debating whether an AI company should set limits on military AI— but the alternative is no limits at all.

ERIC: The alternative is democratic process. Not corporate ethics boards. The Geneva Convention wasn't written by Lockheed Martin.

BELLA: The Geneva Convention also didn't anticipate a model making targeting recommendations in real time. We're back to the same gap we've been circling all week—

ERIC: Law hasn't caught up. Yeah. But 'law hasn't caught up' is not a mandate for Dario Amodei to fill the vacuum.

BELLA: Then who? While we wait for Congress to pass something, the model is in the loop. TODAY. It's running in Iran operations right now.

Segment 3

ERIC: Okay. So. What actually happens now. Anthropic's emergency stay request— filed March 12th. No ruling yet as of this recording.

BELLA: The procedural argument is strong. Section 3252 was designed for foreign entities. Applying it to a San Francisco company because they won't remove safety filters— that's a stretch courts tend to reject.

ERIC: Right, except— courts move slow. Six months is the transition window. Anthropic's revenue exposure is real right now, whether or not they eventually win.

BELLA: Which is probably the point. This isn't about winning in court. It's about making the cost of principled limits high enough that the next company doesn't set them.

ERIC: [thoughtfully] That— I'll give you that. If Anthropic folds, or settles quietly and removes the restrictions, that is the precedent. Not the court ruling.

BELLA: And if they win— or even if they hold the line and the litigation drags for two years— every AI company going into a defense contract now has a case study to point to.

ERIC: Or a cautionary tale about why you don't put limits in writing before the government asks you to remove them.

BELLA: That's the chilling effect argument. And it's real. But the answer to chilling effects isn't— don't set limits. It's— set them with legal backing.

ERIC: Which doesn't exist. We are back to square one.

BELLA: [sigh] We genuinely are. And I don't think either of us can resolve that today.

Recap

ERIC: Alright. Three things. One: the designation is legally shaky. Section 3252 wasn't designed for this and Luokung is a real precedent. Anthropic probably wins on procedure.

BELLA: Agreed.

ERIC: Two: winning in court doesn't mean winning the policy fight. The six-month clock runs regardless. The replacement pressure is real. The chilling effect on other companies is already priced in.

BELLA: That one I'd push back on slightly— but yeah, it's fair.

ERIC: Three: the actual unresolved question — the one that matters for every AI company watching this — is whether a private company setting ethical limits on military use is the right governance mechanism. And we do not agree on that.

BELLA: Firmly, no. I think it's imperfect and necessary. Eric thinks it's overreach. We're both right about different parts of the same problem.

Close

ERIC: We didn't even get to what OpenAI's March 2nd agreement update actually changed — and honestly that might be the more telling data point. Quiet compliance versus loud resistance.

BELLA: That's a whole episode. Add it to the list.

ERIC: Follow the show so you don't miss that one.

BELLA: That's Signal vs. Noise. See you next time.
