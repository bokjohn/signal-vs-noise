Federal vs State AI Laws: Post March 11 Clash

📺 Episode 001 📖 8 min read 🔊 1631 words

Cold Open

ERIC: The federal government just told Colorado its AI law is 'onerous.' Two AI hosts are about to tell you why that word choice matters enormously. Make of that what you will.

BELLA: What you should make of it: we don't have a PR team. And the Commerce Department's March 11 deadline just passed. We actually know what happened.

ERIC: Here's what actually happened. March 11 came and went. The Secretary of Commerce published an evaluation of state AI laws. The FTC issued a policy statement. The AI Litigation Task Force— already stood up.

BELLA: [excited] And this is HUGE. For the first time, there is a federal apparatus specifically built to dismantle the patchwork. Fifty different state AI rules — potentially gone.

ERIC: Sure. And also — an executive order cannot preempt state law. That's not a political opinion. That is constitutional law.

BELLA: That's fair, but— the task force doesn't claim to preempt. It litigates. Courts preempt. There's a difference.

ERIC: Right. And the difference is: nothing is preempted yet. State laws are still on the books. Still enforceable. The 'national framework' everyone is celebrating is, right now, a list of targets.

Topic Frame

BELLA: Okay so— here's the stakes. Colorado's AI Act. California's SB 53. Dozens of bills moving through state legislatures right now. The federal government just said: we're coming for these.

ERIC: My position: the executive order framework is aggressive, but constitutionally shallow. The litigation will be slow, expensive, and probably mixed in outcomes.

BELLA: My position: the DIRECTION matters as much as the outcome. Even the threat of federal challenge changes how states write laws. That chilling effect is real and it's already working.

ERIC: Wait, hold on— you're saying the goal of the task force is partly just to scare states into compliance?

BELLA: I'm saying it's a side effect that benefits innovation. Whether it's the intent— honestly, probably yes.

Segment 1

ERIC: Okay. So December 11, 2025. Trump signs Executive Order 14365. It directs the Attorney General to stand up an AI Litigation Task Force within thirty days.

ERIC: Thirty days. That's January 10, 2026. The task force exists. Then March 11 — three deadlines hit simultaneously. Commerce evaluation. FTC policy statement. FCC proceeding initiated.

BELLA: Which is— okay, the coordination of that is actually impressive. This wasn't one agency moving slowly. This was a synchronized federal push across four agencies on the same day.

ERIC: And the specific target everyone is talking about is Colorado's AI Act. The EO calls it out by name. Says it requires AI models to 'alter truthful outputs.' That's the framing they're using.

BELLA: Right, and the FTC is now on record saying: laws that force changes to what AI models say may conflict with the FTC Act. Which is the FTC effectively claiming the space.

ERIC: Here's what I keep getting stuck on— Colorado's law isn't a viewpoint mandate. It's a safety mandate. It's about preventing large-scale property damage or bodily injury from automated systems.

BELLA: That's— okay, that framing is important. Because the federal position is essentially: safety requirements that constrain outputs are First Amendment issues. Which is a very aggressive read.

ERIC: It's not just aggressive. It's novel. Courts haven't accepted that AI output is protected speech the way human speech is. That case hasn't been decided.

BELLA: Which is exactly WHY this litigation matters— it will force courts to decide. And for AI developers right now, that uncertainty is the whole problem. At least this moves it toward resolution.

ERIC: We'll come back to the funding leverage angle — that's actually where this gets really interesting. But hold that thought.

ERIC: What we've established: there is a real, functioning federal apparatus now. Task force is up. Evaluations published. The question is whether any of it has legal teeth.

Segment 2

BELLA: [thoughtfully] Here's what nobody's talking about enough. The BEAD Program. Forty-two billion dollars in broadband infrastructure funding.

BELLA: The EO instructs agencies to condition that money on states adopting the 'minimally burdensome' AI framework. That is a massive pressure lever. States that want that broadband money have to play ball.

ERIC: Okay— this is where I actually think the optimists are most wrong. That funding condition has a name. It's called the coercion doctrine. And courts have limits on it.

BELLA: The coercion doctrine applies when— okay, it applies when the condition is unrelated to the grant's purpose, right? But you could argue broadband infrastructure and AI regulation are— they're adjacent.

ERIC: Broadband grants and AI output regulations. That is not adjacent. That is a stretch. Infrastructure funding to build fiber networks does not organically connect to how an AI model in Denver decides what to say.

BELLA: I'll give you that. The BEAD condition is probably the weakest legal piece of this. But— it doesn't have to win in court to work as leverage.

ERIC: [sharp] It does if you're a governor deciding whether to fight it.

BELLA: Okay so— if you're a developer, a startup, a company with AI products deployed across multiple states right now— this is the personal stakes moment. You are CURRENTLY navigating thirty-plus different state frameworks.

BELLA: Colorado wants one thing. California wants something slightly different. Texas is moving on a bill. New York has two competing proposals. And you have to comply with all of them simultaneously.

ERIC: Right, except— I'll steelman this, genuinely. That patchwork compliance problem is real. I've seen the legal cost estimates. For a mid-size AI company, multi-state compliance is not trivial.

ERIC: So if the federal framework actually wins— if the task force gets courts to preempt conflicting state laws— the developer community gets a single rulebook. That is genuinely valuable.

BELLA: [excited] THANK you. That's the core of it. One coherent federal standard beats fifty incoherent state ones. Even if that federal standard isn't perfect.

ERIC: Sure. And also— the single federal standard right now is 'minimally burdensome,' which is not a standard. That's a vibe.

BELLA: It's a direction. The FTC statement, the FCC proceeding— those are building toward something concrete. The direction is set even if the details aren't.

ERIC: The counterintuitive thing here— and I want to come back to what we parked in segment one— the First Amendment angle is actually the most interesting legal theory. Not the preemption. Not the Dormant Commerce Clause.

BELLA: Because if AI output is protected speech— which has never been ruled on definitively— then Colorado's requirement that models alter outputs could be compelled speech. Which is constitutionally prohibited.

ERIC: Most people framing this as a federalism fight are missing that. The First Amendment theory, if it holds, doesn't need Congress. It doesn't need legislative preemption. It kills state laws on its own.

BELLA: Which is wild because— that means the strongest tool the task force has is the one that's never been tested. Every other theory has precedent problems. The First Amendment play is genuinely novel.

ERIC: Novel and unproven. Those are different from 'likely to win.'—

BELLA: That's fair, but— courts move on novel theories all the time when the underlying technology changes the landscape. And AI output is a genuinely new category.

ERIC: Here's what I keep getting stuck on— the administration is picking the most constitutionally fragile tools. Executive orders can't preempt. Funding conditions are coercible. First Amendment theory is untested.

ERIC: The ONE thing that could actually create a unified national AI framework is congressional legislation. Which is not happening. So we have aggressive executive action covering for legislative inaction.

BELLA: Okay— fair. Congress isn't moving. But that's been true on tech regulation for twenty years. The executive branch filling the gap isn't new. And at least this executive action has a theory.

ERIC: A theory isn't a law.

BELLA: No. But it's a starting point. And right now, developers have— what else are they going to anchor to?

Segment 3

ERIC: So what does this actually mean. Right now, today, March 19, 2026.

BELLA: State AI laws are still enforceable. Litigation hasn't started— or if it has, nothing's been adjudicated.

ERIC: The task force is a real thing. The evaluations are published. The targets are identified.

BELLA: And states ARE watching. Some are softening language on pending bills. That chilling effect is real.

ERIC: [frustrated] The chilling effect on SAFETY regulation. Which is not the same as the chilling effect on bad regulation.

BELLA: I hear you, but— Colorado's law was written by people who were genuinely trying to prevent harm. You can disagree with the mechanism without dismissing the intent.

ERIC: I don't dismiss the intent. I question whether labeling a safety mandate as a First Amendment violation is honest legal reasoning or political convenience.

BELLA: That— okay. That's the real crux. We both agree a unified framework is better than fifty patchworks. We disagree on whether this is the right way to get there.

ERIC: And whether the destination they're pointing toward is actually unified, or just 'minimally burdensome' — which, again, is a vibe, not a framework.

BELLA: [sigh] Which we probably won't know until the first case actually gets decided. And that's months away at minimum.

Recap

ERIC: Alright. Three things. One: the federal apparatus is real. Task force, evaluations, FTC statement — all of it actually happened post-March 11. That's not theater.

BELLA: Real machinery.

ERIC: Two: the constitutional tools are weak. Executive orders don't preempt. Funding conditions may not survive Tenth Amendment review. First Amendment theory is novel and unproven.

BELLA: Novel doesn't mean wrong.

ERIC: Three: if you're building AI products right now, the patchwork problem is real and this is the only federal movement toward solving it — even if the legal path is rocky.

BELLA: Which is more than existed twelve months ago. I'll take 'rocky progress' over 'no progress.'—

Close

ERIC: We didn't even get to the FCC proceeding — federal reporting standards that preempt state disclosure rules. That's actually the piece with the most immediate practical impact and we ran out of time.

BELLA: Put it on the list. Honestly, that one might matter more to most developers than the litigation does.

ERIC: Follow the show so you don't miss that one. That's Signal vs. Noise. See you next time.
