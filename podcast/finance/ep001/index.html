MARCUS: Call it a range-bound market looking for a catalyst.

JAMES: Yields are the story underneath all of this. The 10-year Treasury is holding in a range that reflects exactly what the Fed is telegraphing — no cuts imminent, no hikes coming.

MARCUS: Fed funds rate: still parked at 3.5 to 3.75 percent. That's where it's been since January 29th when the FOMC met and held. Two dissenters — Miran and Waller — wanted a 25 basis point cut. The committee said no.

JAMES: Dollar has been interesting — tariffs typically support a stronger dollar through reduced import demand, but the growth uncertainty is creating a ceiling.

Segment 1

MARCUS: Alright. Here's the bull case, and it starts with something almost nobody is talking about.

MARCUS: The Budget Lab at Yale ran the numbers on March 9th and here's what they found: if Section 122 tariffs expire after 150 days — which is the scheduled outcome — the total price level impact on consumers is 0.3 to 0.4 percent. That's it.

MARCUS: In dollar terms, that's a $450 to $570 loss per average household in 2025 dollars. Now that sounds like real money — and it is for individual families.

MARCUS: But from a MACRO standpoint, from a portfolio impact standpoint, that is not an economy-breaking number. That is a rounding error in the context of GDP.

JAMES: Okay but you're cherry-picking the expiration scenario. What if they don't expire?

MARCUS: I'll get there — let me finish the setup. Even in the permanent scenario, the Budget Lab projects 0.6 to 0.7 percent price level impact, $770 to $940 per household.

MARCUS: That's not nothing, but it's also not the hyperinflationary spiral that the bears have been predicting. The Fed's own January minutes said the pass-through to customers has been LOWER than anticipated.

MARCUS: Here's what everyone's missing — the Fed's January 28th FOMC minutes project real GDP outpacing potential growth all the way through 2028. Not just surviving tariffs. OUTPACING potential.

MARCUS: The language in those minutes is explicit: the drag from higher tariffs is expected to WANE. That is not a committee that's scared.

JAMES: Or it's a committee that's optimistic in its projections and has been wrong before. The Fed projected no significant tariff inflation in early 2025 and then we hit 14.3% effective rates.

MARCUS: Fair point on the 2025 peak — but the IEEPA tariffs that caused that spike got struck down. We're now at 10.5% and scheduled to come down. The direction of travel matters.

MARCUS: And here's the piece that directly connects to your 401k: Powell said in his March press conference — on record — that there are NO rate hikes planned. None.

MARCUS: Think about what that means for equity valuations. The discount rate stays anchored. Your S&P 500 holdings, your tech positions, your industrials — they're being valued against a stable rate environment.

MARCUS: That is the single most important input for equity prices right now, and the Fed just told you it's not moving against you.

JAMES: He also said tariffs pose a MORE significant inflation risk than oil prices. Those were his exact words. You can't have it both ways — you can't cite Powell as your bull catalyst and ignore what he actually said about the risk.

MARCUS: [excited] And THAT is exactly the nuance I want to drill into — because Powell acknowledged the risk and STILL said no hikes. That's the tell.

MARCUS: When a central banker sees a risk and explicitly takes the hawkish tool off the table, that is a green light for risk assets. The Fed is absorbing the tariff shock so the market doesn't have to.

MARCUS: The March SEP — the dot plot — kept 2026 forecasts essentially unchanged from December. One quarter-point cut projected for this year, another in 2027. Long-run rate nudged up slightly to 3.1%.

MARCUS: That is a committee that looked at 10.5% tariffs, oil up 50%, an Iran conflict — and barely flinched. That's not silence. That's confidence.

JAMES: [thoughtfully] I'll give you the dot plot stability — that's a real data point. But let me tell you what I think you're missing, and it's buried in a paper the Fed published on March 5th.

MARCUS: The 'Slow Climb' paper. Go ahead.

Segment 2

JAMES: Right. 'The Slow Climb: How Tariffs Gradually Raised Retail Prices in 2025.' The Fed's own researchers used micro data — store-level pricing data — to show that tariff pass-through to consumers is DELAYED. It doesn't hit immediately. It creeps.

JAMES: And if you believe that paper, we may not have seen the full inflation impact of the current tariff regime yet.

JAMES: Here's the risk nobody's fully pricing in: the Fed is operating on a model that assumes tariff inflation is transitory and front-loaded.

JAMES: But if the 'Slow Climb' dynamic is real — if retailers and supply chains are still absorbing costs that will eventually get passed through — then the Fed's 'mid-2026 inflation waning' projection could be wrong.

MARCUS: But the January minutes specifically said pass-through has been LOWER than anticipated — that's the opposite of your slow climb argument—

JAMES: Let me finish — the point is those two findings aren't contradictory. Lower-than-expected pass-through in the near term is exactly what you'd see in the early stages of a slow climb. Businesses absorb cost shocks in the short run.

JAMES: Then they pass them through when margins get squeezed enough. That's the historical pattern.

JAMES: And here's the historical parallel that should make every 401k holder uncomfortable: the last time we had an effective tariff rate at these levels — pre-1943 territory — the economy was in a fundamentally different structural position.

JAMES: We weren't running the kind of services-dominant, consumer-debt-heavy economy we have today. The transmission mechanisms are different.

JAMES: The Fed's own survey from March 5th described small businesses calling these tariffs a 'gut punch.' That's not analyst language — that's Main Street language. Small businesses are raising prices.

JAMES: And small businesses are the leading edge of consumer price inflation, because they have less pricing power and less ability to absorb costs than large caps.

MARCUS: Small business sentiment is always volatile. You can't build a rate forecast on survey language.

JAMES: [quietly] Marcus. Look at the yield curve. Look at what two Fed governors — Miran and Waller — did in January. They dissented. They wanted to cut. The committee overruled them.

JAMES: You have internal disagreement at the Fed about whether the economy is strong enough to hold at these rates, and you're reading that as confidence?

MARCUS: [thoughtfully] That's— actually a fair read of the dissent. Two members thought the data supported a cut, the majority disagreed. You can read that as division.

JAMES: I read it as: the dovish case exists inside the Fed right now. And if the slow-climb inflation data starts coming in hotter than projected through Q2, that dovish case evaporates. You don't get the cuts that the market is pricing in.

JAMES: And THAT is when equity valuations start to crack.

JAMES: Here's the other piece. The Budget Lab's post-substitution effective rate is 9.1% — not 10.5%. Why? Because importers and consumers are already substituting away from tariffed goods.

JAMES: That substitution behavior is itself deflationary in some sectors but inflationary in others, as domestic alternatives cost more. The net effect is messy and the Fed knows it.

MARCUS: Okay, I'll give you the substitution complexity — that's a real wrinkle. The 9.1% post-substitution versus 10.5% headline is a genuine analytical gap and the net sectoral effects are hard to model cleanly.

MARCUS: That's a legitimate concern for precision forecasting.

JAMES: And the USTR's 2026 Trade Policy Agenda is doubling down on 'America First' — supplemental tariffs on trading partners, more domestic production mandates. So the policy direction isn't toward de-escalation.

JAMES: The 150-day clock on Section 122 tariffs might reset, not expire. That's a scenario the market is not pricing.

MARCUS: [frustrated] You're stacking worst-case scenarios. The scheduled expiration IS the base case until there's evidence otherwise. You can't trade on hypothetical extensions.

JAMES: You trade on risk-adjusted outcomes. And the risk of non-expiration is non-trivial given the stated policy direction. That's the footnote you keep skipping. Bottom line — the bear case isn't that the economy collapses.

JAMES: It's that the Fed's hands stay tied longer than the market expects, cuts get pushed out, and valuations compress quietly.
