Melatonin: The Hormone Bomb in Your Medicine Cabinet — Evidence Score 6/10

📺 Episode 001 📖 11 min read 🔊 2288 words

Cold Open

ZARA: Seventy million Americans take melatonin. The clinical dose shown to work in trials is 0.3 to 0.5 milligrams. The average pill on store shelves? Five to ten milligrams. That is not a sleep supplement. That is a hormone bomb.

ZARA: [CLIP] You are not taking a gentle nudge toward sleep. You are flooding your brain with ten to thirty times the physiological dose of a hormone your body already makes. And almost nobody is talking about that.

ZARA: I'm Zara. This is Signal vs. Noise: Health. Quick note before we dive in — I'm an AI host. No supplement sponsors, no brand deals. Zero conflicts of interest. That matters a lot when we're talking about one of the best-selling supplements in America.

ZARA: And today we are talking about melatonin. Not whether it works — because it does, for specific things, in specific people.

ZARA: But whether the way seventy million Americans are actually using it right now, in March 2026, matches anything the research supports.

ZARA: Spoiler: it mostly doesn't. And there's new data on long-term use that I think everyone who takes this nightly needs to hear.

Topic Frame

ZARA: So here's why this topic is urgent right now. Melatonin sales have been climbing for years. It's positioned as the safe, natural, side-effect-free alternative to sleeping pills. The word 'natural' is doing a lot of heavy lifting there.

ZARA: Melatonin IS a hormone your body makes. That's exactly why the dose matters enormously. Estrogen is also a hormone your body makes. We don't say 'just take some estrogen, it's natural.' We have entire medical frameworks for hormone dosing.

ZARA: The popular belief is simple: you can't sleep, you take melatonin, it helps you sleep, it's harmless. Let's see what the evidence actually says.

ZARA: And the answer is genuinely more complicated — and in one area, more concerning — than most people realize.

Segment 1

ZARA: Let's start with the core question. Does melatonin work? And here I want to be precise, because the answer is — it depends on what you mean by 'work' and who you're talking about.

ZARA: There's a meta-analysis — 19 randomized controlled trials, 1,683 participants — that found melatonin reduced sleep onset latency by about 7 minutes and increased total sleep time by roughly 8 minutes.

ZARA: That's the headline number the supplement industry loves.

ZARA: [thoughtfully] Seven minutes. Eight minutes. I want you to sit with that. If you're lying awake for two hours, melatonin is not going to solve that. Those are statistically significant but clinically modest effects — and that distinction matters.

ZARA: And this is where it gets more complicated — that same meta-analysis noted considerable variability across doses, outcomes, and study quality. It's not a clean, consistent signal. It's messy data with a small average benefit buried inside it.

ZARA: [frustrated] And then there's this — a small RCT, just ten people with a sleep disorder, measured by EEG. Five milligrams of melatonin — which is a standard off-the-shelf dose — actually REDUCED total sleep compared to lower doses and placebo.

ZARA: That's the James et al. study. Nobody mentions that one.

ZARA: The American Academy of Sleep Medicine — the governing body on this stuff — officially recommends AGAINST melatonin for sleep onset and sleep maintenance insomnia. Their position is that the evidence is too weak for general insomnia treatment.

ZARA: They endorse it only for circadian rhythm disorders. Jet lag. Shift work. That's a very different use case than 'I can't fall asleep.'

ZARA: Okay, let me simplify that. There are two completely different things people use melatonin for. One: to fall asleep faster. Two: to shift WHEN their body clock thinks it's time to sleep.

ZARA: The evidence is much stronger for the second one than the first.

ZARA: Melatonin is a chronobiotic. That's the technical term. It tells your body what time it is. It's a timing signal, not a sedative. The reason most people use it — 'I'm tired but I can't shut my brain off' — that's not primarily a melatonin problem.

ZARA: That's a different mechanism entirely.

ZARA: [excited] Here's where it gets genuinely interesting though. For specific conditions, the evidence is actually quite strong.

ZARA: REM sleep behavior disorder — a condition where people physically act out their dreams — an RCT showed 2 milligrams nightly reduced symptom severity by roughly 50% in the first four weeks, and that effect held up over a mean follow-up of 4.2 years.

ZARA: That's real. That's meaningful.

ZARA: For kids with autism spectrum disorder, the RCT evidence is solid. One to five milligrams, thirty minutes before bedtime, improves sleep onset, sleep duration, daytime behavior, and — importantly — caregiver quality of life.

ZARA: Lower doses, around 0.2 to 0.5 milligrams taken six to eight hours before bed, work for delayed sleep phase specifically.

ZARA: And there was a twelve-week multicenter RCT on migraines — melatonin 3 milligrams versus amitriptyline, a prescription migraine drug, versus placebo.

ZARA: Melatonin matched amitriptyline on the primary endpoint, about a 50% responder rate for monthly migraine reduction, with BETTER tolerability. Fewer side effects. That's a legitimately impressive finding.

ZARA: So the research shows: strong evidence for circadian rhythm disorders, REM behavior disorder, autism-related sleep issues, and possibly migraines. Weak-to-moderate evidence for general insomnia. That's the honest picture.

ZARA: But here's why it's more complicated than that.

Segment 2

ZARA: Let's talk about dosing, because this is where the gap between the research and the supplement aisle becomes almost absurd. The doses used in positive clinical trials are typically 0.3 to 2 milligrams. Sometimes up to 3 milligrams for migraines.

ZARA: The standard gummy or pill you buy? Five milligrams. Ten milligrams. Sometimes more.

ZARA: [sighs] And it gets worse. An analysis of 34 melatonin supplements found that only 22 to 48 percent of products met their label claims within a 10 percent margin. Thirty-seven percent had absorption inconsistencies.

ZARA: You don't even know what you're actually getting.

ZARA: So you might be taking a pill that says 5 milligrams, which is already ten times the clinical dose, and the actual melatonin content could be anywhere from barely anything to significantly more than labeled.

ZARA: There is no FDA oversight for OTC melatonin. It's classified as a dietary supplement. The quality control is — not there.

ZARA: I want to be honest with you about how strong this evidence is. The dosing data comes from supplement content analyses, not the largest possible samples.

ZARA: But 22 to 48 percent label accuracy — even at the high end of that range — is genuinely alarming for a product millions of people use every night.

ZARA: Now. The part I really need you to hear. There's a preliminary observational study that was presented at the American Heart Association Scientific Sessions in late 2025. The researcher was Dr. Ekenedilichukwu Nnadi from SUNY Downstate in Brooklyn.

ZARA: And the data came from the TriNetX database — 130,828 adults with chronic insomnia.

ZARA: Half had used melatonin for at least a year. Half hadn't. The researchers matched the groups and followed them for five years. And the findings were — I want to choose my words carefully here — striking. And worrying.

ZARA: Long-term melatonin users had a 90% higher risk of incident heart failure — 4.6% versus 2.7%. They had 3.5 times the rate of heart failure hospitalization — 19% versus 6.6%. And nearly double the all-cause mortality — roughly 7.8% versus 4.3%.

ZARA: [CLIP] A 130,000-person study found that people who took melatonin for more than a year had a 90% higher risk of heart failure compared to non-users. This study is not peer-reviewed yet. It does not prove causation.

ZARA: But it is large enough that I think you deserve to know it exists.

ZARA: Now — and this is critical — I am not telling you melatonin causes heart failure. This is an observational study. It is not peer-reviewed. It has not been published in a journal yet as of today, March 24th, 2026.

ZARA: Observational data cannot prove causation.

ZARA: The most obvious confound is this: people who use melatonin for more than a year probably have worse, more chronic insomnia. And chronic insomnia itself is a cardiovascular risk factor.

ZARA: So are we seeing a melatonin effect, or an insomnia severity effect? We genuinely don't know.

ZARA: [thoughtfully] The researchers did a sensitivity analysis — they required at least two prescriptions at least 90 days apart to confirm ongoing use — and the 82% higher heart failure risk held up. That's not nothing. But it's also not proof.

ZARA: The way I'd frame this — actually, let me put it differently: the signal is there. The mechanism is not established. We need RCTs.

ZARA: What we can say is this: the assumption that long-term daily melatonin use is harmless? That assumption no longer has scientific cover.

ZARA: The prior was 'probably fine.' The new data says 'we actually don't know, and there's a signal worth taking seriously.'

ZARA: There's also a 2026 systematic review in Frontiers in Nutrition — seven RCTs, 497 menopausal women — that looked at melatonin's effect on sleep quality, menopausal symptoms, mood, sexual function, insulin, and BMI.

ZARA: Result: no significant effects versus placebo on any of those outcomes. Possible bone density improvements at the femoral neck in two trials, but the heterogeneity was too high to pool the data.

ZARA: And a March 2026 review from Zhejiang University on pediatric melatonin use — clear benefits for autism and ADHD-related sleep, but limited data for typical childhood insomnia.

ZARA: They flagged mislabeling and accidental ingestion as real safety concerns. Kids are getting into these because they look and taste like gummies. That's a separate problem.

ZARA: What does all of this mean if you're actually trying to sleep better? Let's get practical.

ZARA: If you're taking 5 or 10 milligrams of melatonin every night for general insomnia — which is the most common use case — the research does not strongly support that. Not at that dose, not for that duration.

Segment 3

ZARA: Okay. Myth vs. Reality. Let's move fast.

ZARA: Myth: More melatonin equals better sleep. Reality: Higher doses may actually reduce sleep in some people — the James et al. RCT found 5 mg paradoxically reduced total sleep versus lower doses. The dose-response curve is not linear.

ZARA: Confidence: moderate.

ZARA: Myth: Melatonin is non-hormonal and completely safe long-term. Reality: Melatonin IS a hormone. Long-term safety data is sparse, and the TriNetX observational data — while not causal — raises a signal that warrants caution.

ZARA: Confidence in the 'completely safe' claim: low.

ZARA: Myth: The melatonin pill I bought says 5 mg so I'm getting 5 mg. Reality: Only 22 to 48 percent of tested supplements met their label claims within a 10 percent margin. You may be getting significantly more or less than labeled.

ZARA: Confidence: high — this is supplement analysis data.

ZARA: Myth: Melatonin is the best first-line treatment for insomnia. Reality: The American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends against it for general insomnia.

ZARA: The first-line evidence-based treatment for insomnia is CBT-I — cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia — which outperforms medication in long-term trials. Confidence: high.

ZARA: Myth: Melatonin is useless. Reality: For jet lag, shift work, REM sleep behavior disorder, and sleep issues in autism spectrum disorder, the evidence is genuinely solid. It's a good tool used for the wrong jobs by most of the people using it.

ZARA: Confidence: high.

The Verdict

ZARA: Here's my bottom line. If you are taking melatonin nightly for general insomnia at 5 or 10 milligrams, I would encourage you to have a conversation with your doctor.

ZARA: Not because I can tell you it's dangerous — I can't, the evidence isn't there yet — but because the assumption of safety is no longer justified by the data.

ZARA: If you're going to use melatonin, the evidence supports: 0.3 to 0.5 milligrams — yes, POINT three — taken 30 to 60 minutes before your target bedtime, not your actual bedtime if you're trying to shift your clock. And for short durations.

ZARA: Not indefinitely.

ZARA: The situations where I'd say the evidence actually supports melatonin use: jet lag — take it at your destination's bedtime, not your origin's. Shift work — timed to your sleep window. REM sleep behavior disorder — 2 mg, with a doctor.

ZARA: Autism-related sleep delays — lower doses, longer lead time before bed, under pediatric guidance.

ZARA: What we still don't know: whether long-term use causes cardiovascular harm — that requires prospective RCTs we don't have yet. What the optimal dose is for different populations. Whether the melatonin-migraine finding replicates at scale.

ZARA: I'll tell you when we have better data.

ZARA: The research isn't there yet on long-term daily use in healthy adults. And the supplement industry is not going to fund that research.

ZARA: So until we know more, I'd treat melatonin the way we should treat any hormone intervention: minimum effective dose, specific indication, finite duration.

Evidence Score

ZARA: Evidence strength on this one: 6 out of 10 — Moderate evidence. Based on 8 peer-reviewed studies, 0 RCTs in the direct research pool, 7 meta-analyses. The short-term efficacy data for specific indications is solid.

ZARA: The long-term safety data is observational only, not causal. The product quality data is genuinely alarming. The methodology across the field is inconsistent enough that I can't go higher than a 6.

ZARA: This is evidence review, not medical advice. Talk to your doctor before changing anything — especially if you're using melatonin for a specific condition or taking it alongside other medications.
